Land Selection Framework

How to arrive

Arriving is a relationship. It starts before you look at any land and it goes on for decades. These four stages are a way of asking, in order, what a place and its people are owed. They do not tell you where to go, and some answers will be: not there.

This page is not a checklist you can finish. The tool filters regions and reads them closely; it does not decide for anyone. Where arriving in a place would harm the community already there, the honest answer is not to go. Where that is the answer, the tool has done its job.

A bioregion is a place known by its water, its living systems and the people who belong to it. An ecoregion line is a scientific classification, drawn by others, and it is not the boundary of anyone's home. Both are here to help a reader see the place before the administrative map.

Choosing a region adds what the project has read about that place: whose land it is, the public bodies to read first, and the lawful way in. Nothing is stored.

Stage one of four

iBefore you look

Begin with who is arriving and why, and with who is already there. A place is known first through the people who hold it, and only then through its climate and its soil.

Questions to carry

  1. Who would arrive together, and for how long are you ready to stay? A route that is open to a family or a group is often closed to one buyer, and the reverse.
  2. Whose land is this, and what do they say about it in their own words?
  3. What is the place already doing without you: returning land, repopulating a village, keeping a commons?
  4. Which language does the place work in, and can you work in it?
  5. If the only thing drawing you is a low price or a mild climate, what is the place getting from your arrival?

Look for

The host community's own pages (listed as first conversations on each region page); a published way of approaching them, where one exists; land that is being returned or repurchased.

Not there if

  • You would be choosing on price or climate alone.
  • The place is mid-way through a land return.
  • The people there have asked outsiders not to come.

Stage two of four

iiWhen you visit

Visit as a guest, through a door the place has opened, and keep the visit small enough that the place can say no.

Questions to carry

  1. Is there a visiting route the place itself publishes (a newcomers' page, a visit programme, a regional welcome service)? Use that one.
  2. Who has been asked, and who has not? Whose voice is missing from the rooms you are shown?
  3. What is not for sale, not for visiting, not for photographing?
  4. Where does your money go while you are there, and who is paid for your time?
  5. Can you stay through more than one season before anything is decided?

Look for

What neighbours say unprompted; who repairs the road, the ditch, the school; housing that local households are looking for.

Not there if

  • You are told no, or told to wait, and the visit goes ahead anyway.
  • You would be the reason a local household loses a rental.

Stage three of four

iiiBefore you commit

Commit means a lease, a membership, a job or, last, a purchase. Many of the routes on the region pages are not purchases. The lawful route and the relational route should be the same route.

Questions to carry

  1. Who holds first claim here (family, neighbours, co-owners, a commons, an assembly), and have you let them use it?
  2. Does any part of your plan depend on a front-owner, a paper residence or a workaround? If so, it is not the route.
  3. What will you write down, with the people already there, about what you will give and what you will not take?
  4. Who can say no at this stage, and how will you hear it?
  5. Have you taken local legal advice in the local language? (The site gives context, not legal advice.)

Look for

Published rules read from the source (statute, commune, nation, trust); a named body that decides; a written understanding both sides can read.

Not there if

  • The land is named for return or under negotiation.
  • The seller is not the community's seller.
  • Your arrival depends on a route designed for someone else.

Stage four of four

ivThe first years

Standing is given, not claimed. For the first years the work is to be present, to carry a share of what the place carries, and to let others decide when you belong.

Questions to carry

  1. What are you doing in the commons: the ditch, the village association, the school, the forest?
  2. Are you giving back in the form the place asks for, not the form you prefer?
  3. What has changed since you arrived: rents, prices, who has left?
  4. What would you do if your presence turned out to be harming the place? (Leaving, or giving land back, are both on the table.)
  5. Over fifty to a hundred years, who will hold what you start?

Look for

Invitations to take on a duty before invitations to speak; people who stay on after the novelty.

Not there if

  • You keep a calendar apart from the village.
  • Prices and rents near you are rising and people who were there before you are leaving.

Who made this

The framework was originated by Askja and is developed by a small working group of practitioners and researchers. The demonstration site was built by one member of that group, who holds the practitioner seat.

There is no public contact route yet; the working group will set one.

How to read it

  • Thresholds filter; they never score or rank.
  • Land standing is qualitative and never a filter.
  • Every value carries its source and vintage.

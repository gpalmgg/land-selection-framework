Methodology

How the framework decides what to look at, and what to leave aside.

This is a designed demonstration of the framework, not a finished data product. The choices it embeds are deliberate: eight criteria, native units rather than normalised scores, state paired with trajectory, and a source, a vintage and a licence beside every value. The points below are the operating commitments behind the demonstration. The rules they apply are written out, each with a test, in the group's FRAMEWORK document (rules F-1 to F-60).

Why eight criteria, and what sits outside them.

The group's starting inventory is Askja's twelve metrics. The demonstration holds the eight that reduce to a sourced number in a native unit at regional scale, each with a threshold the viewer can set: climate trajectory, water stress, soil organic carbon, forest-cover trajectory, solar-energy potential, conflict proximity, regenerative-network density and population density. They share the one property that makes them filterable: each is a sourced number in a native unit. Each criterion keeps the number of its metric in Askja's set, printed in the grid below; the numbers identify metrics and say nothing about importance. Each axis can end a project on its own, which is why none is folded into another.

Three further things sit beside the eight sliders and are not numbers on a scale. First, seven per-jurisdiction layers (legal ownership, land cost, hospital access, demographic trajectory, soil contamination, water source control and climate buffering) appear on every region page as qualitative records, each with its source, its confidence and its gaps. Second, four categorical filters on the home page read those records: Foreign ownership, Affordability, Climate buffering and Legal direction. They filter the way the sliders do: a region either matches the option chosen or it does not, and nothing is added up. Third, every region carries a Land standing entry and a legal pathway, described under ethics below, which are never scored and never filtered.

Legal and cost questions come first in practice: before soil or climate matter, a reader has to know who may buy, what may be built and what the land costs, and the region pages carry those records for that reason. That is an observation about how arriving works. It is not a ranking: the framework assigns no priority between criteria, and every slider and filter is independent of the others. A categorical fact such as who may buy land in a given country is not a point on a scale, and forcing it onto a slider would be the false quantification the framework refuses.

Other criteria were considered and are out, for stated reasons. Cultural and lifestyle fit is out because the group's Decision 6 puts subjective dimensions outside V1: they do not live in datasets. Two things people often fold into fit are carried elsewhere on purpose. Language and the manner of entry are written into each region's Land standing entry as sourced prose (the Galway Gaeltacht entry, for instance, names the Irish-language conditions of the county plan). A composite coherence score is out under the no-composite rule below. The pruning principle is unchanged: keep as a filter only what reduces to a checkable number, and hold everything else as sourced prose.

Each criterion is measured in its own native unit, drawn from a named public dataset. State paired with trajectory.

A current value tells the practitioner what a region is now; a trajectory tells them where it is heading. The framework holds the two side by side rather than folding one into the other. Every cell carries a state and, where the data allow one, a trajectory: a direction (rising, falling or steady), the size of the change, and the rule that produced it. Five criteria carry a direction (climate trajectory, water stress, forest cover trajectory, conflict proximity and population density). Three carry a state only (soil organic carbon, solar PV potential and regenerative network density), and the grid says why: there is no open regional trend dataset for soil carbon, the solar product is a long-term average, and the network count is a single baseline wave. Where a direction is a projection, the cell says so and names the scenario (F-6).

Climate is the case where state and trajectory sit closest. The value on the dashboard is not today's weather but the mid-century projection: the 2041–2060 mean annual air temperature from the 17-model WorldClim CMIP6 ensemble under SSP2-4.5, averaged over the region's footprint. Alentejo reads 18.3°C, West Cork and Connemara 11.1°C and Transylvania 9.3°C, each a projected state that already encodes the warming a fifty-year project is planning into. The trajectory field gives the change against WorldClim's 1970–2000 over the same cells: 2°C, 1.3°C and 3°C.

Water stress is a ratio, and the page says what kind. WRI's Aqueduct 4.0 technical note defines baseline water stress as the ratio of total water demand to available renewable surface and groundwater supplies, calculated for each HydroBASINS level-6 sub-basin, and converts the raw ratio to risk categories and to a 0–5 score. The dashboard shows the raw ratio for the 2050 business-as-usual scenario (SSP3-7.0), area-weighted over the sub-basins of the footprint, with WRI's own class names (low under 0.1 up to extremely high above 0.8). It is not a rescaled score of the framework's own, and it passes 1 where demand exceeds supply: Alentejo reads 1.505, Transylvania 0.415 and Connemara 0.011. The cell stores the 2050 value only. The baseline ratio (1979–2019) is not stored as a second value; the trajectory field stores the change from it, and the two scenario families (climate on SSP2-4.5, water on SSP3-7.0) are labelled as different futures.

Forest cover is the pure-trend case, where the number is the trajectory. Hansen Global Forest Change tracks tree cover at 30-metre resolution, and the framework reads it as net change per decade: gain recorded for 2000–2012 minus gross loss for 2001–2023, as a share of the year-2000 canopy. Alentejo reads −11.2% per decade, West Cork and Connemara −3.2% and Transylvania −1.5%. Under this definition every footprint on the slate reads below zero, because Hansen records gain only to 2012, counts harvest and fire as loss and does not count regrowth after 2012; the number says that canopy was lost and cannot say why. It is a trajectory to read with land-use history, not a verdict on a forest.

No composite scoring.

One of the working group's non-negotiables is that the framework never produces a single composite score across criteria (F-1, F-2). A composite requires coefficients: how much rainfall offsets a degree of warming, how much cheap land offsets a thin civil-society stack. Once those coefficients live inside the framework, the framework is making the decision instead of the practitioner. Composites also let a region with one fatal flaw look acceptable because compensating values average it away, which is exactly the failure mode for fifty-year decisions where no flaw is averageable. Alentejo makes the point concretely: its solar resource reads 1,643 kWh/kWp/yr (Global Solar Atlas, long-term average) while its 2050 water-stress ratio reads 1.505. A composite would let the sun pull the water problem back toward the middle of a range, yet a settlement there has to close its water budget whatever the sun gives it. Averaging buries the one number that matters. The alternative is filtering (F-3): each criterion has a threshold the viewer sets, regions within every threshold stay visible and regions outside any one threshold dim. The shape of the result is a smaller set, never a ranked list.

Native units throughout.

Temperature stays in degrees Celsius, soil organic carbon in g/kg, water stress as WRI's ratio, forest change in percent per decade, solar output in kWh/kWp/yr, conflict as an event count and population as persons per km². Land prices are not a criterion: they sit in the land-cost record of each region page, in the currency of the source and with its year. The framework refuses to reshape every variable onto one normalised grid, because reshaping costs information (F-4). The modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP), familiar to anyone who has worked with spatial data, has two parts: the scale effect, where statistics change as the unit of aggregation changes, and the zoning effect, where they change as the boundaries shift. Keeping each variable in its native unit, at the scale where it makes sense, is the discipline the framework chose over visual neatness.

In practice each criterion is read at the resolution its source actually resolves, from a 30 m forest raster to level-6 river sub-basins (the grid above gives each native unit). Reshaping them all onto one universal grid, an H3 hexagon say, would either blur the 30 m forest signal or invent 30 m detail for a climate model that never produced it, and either way manufacture a precision the data does not hold. The zoning effect bites just as hard. Alentejo's footprint mean for water stress hides a spread: its sub-basins run from 0.14 to 2.44, which is why the cell records the range in its label. The aggregation scale is not a cosmetic choice; it changes the number the practitioner reads.

Six of eight criteria describe the past or the present. The furthest any of them looks is 2060, a projection. The tool reads what it can, and says plainly where it cannot see.

Every value the framework presents carries three things. A source, a named public dataset. A vintage, the year or scenario the value represents: "2041–2060, SSP2-4.5" for a climate projection, "2050 business-as-usual" for water stress, "2001–2023" for a forest trend. And a licence, the terms under which the data may be reused. The licence is stated per source, and per value only where a cell cites a source of its own; the source list below records, for each source, the page that states its licence. Where the provider's page states none, or could not be opened, the entry reads "licence not confirmed", and a licence is never inferred from a dataset's reputation. Most of the numerical backbone is CC BY 4.0. WorldClim is free for non-commercial use only. Two map layers are non-commercial (GEM seismic hazard and EOxCloudless imagery) and the Esri layers are under Esri's licence, so a commercial use would need a licence or a swap; the group has no commercial product (F-5).

Two levels of sourcing are worth telling apart. The criterion-level source is the dataset named in the grid above. The cell-level source is what a single value cites. In the current data the two are the same for every cell: each of the eight criteria is computed for every region from one dataset, over a footprint recorded for that region (an administrative polygon, a bounding box or a disc, whichever the cell names), by scripts the group runs by hand. The regional and national sources a reader will meet elsewhere, statutes, planning plans and statistical offices, back the Land standing, legal-pathway and land-cost records, and each is listed under the criterion or layer it serves.

On a fifty-to-hundred-year decision, vintage and licence are not bureaucratic footnotes, they are the point. A climate projection is a model run that will be re-run: newer ensembles, revised pathways and better downscaling will move the numbers, and a value stamped "2041–2060, SSP2-4.5, WorldClim CMIP6" tells a community exactly which run to re-check it against. The group keeps per-region research notes; the sources they cite are listed here. No value without a source, no source without a vintage, no claim that cannot be re-checked: where two sources disagree the cell says so, and where a value could not be reproduced it is left as a stated gap rather than a number. Transparency is what lets a place that will outlive this dataset check it for itself.

The 23 map layers, and the gaps not shipped.

The interactive map carries 23 toggleable layers, grouped into six themes. Seventeen are live public services, each with its own attribution, read in the visitor's browser, so the provider can change or withdraw them. Six are processed files the site serves itself. WorldClim is a criterion source, not a map layer: the map draws no temperature layer from it.

  • Climate & water: Precipitation — live service; Water stress 2050 — processed file; Water depletion 2050 — processed file; Climate zones 1991-2020 (Köppen-Geiger) — processed file; Climate zones 2041-2070 (Köppen-Geiger, SSP2-4.5) — processed file.
  • Land & soil: Forest loss — live service; Soil organic carbon — live service; Land cover (10m) — live service; Land cover, ESA WorldCover 2021 (10 m) — live service.
  • Energy: Solar PV potential — live service.
  • Hazards: Coastal flood / SLR — live service; River flood depth, 1-in-50-year (1960-1999) — live service; River flood depth, 2050 (SSP2/RCP4.5) — live service; River flood depth, 2050 (SSP3/RCP8.5) — live service; Seismic hazard — live service.
  • People & access: Population density — live service; Travel time to cities — live service; Conflict density — processed file; Ecovillage sites — processed file.
  • Terrain & imagery: Terrain relief — live service; Topographic map — live service; Recent satellite — live service; Night lights — live service.

What the map does not ship is as honest as what it does. Layers that were probed and did not hold up are retired and recorded in the layer registry with the reason: a drought indicator that covers Europe only, surface-temperature layers whose units could not be confirmed, a flood layer that rendered as a flat colour, and an aridity index that covers Australia only. Aridity is carried by the Köppen climate-zone layers instead. Rather than fake a layer with a proxy dressed up as the real thing, the map leaves it out and says so.

What this demonstration is, and is not

A designed demonstration of the framework, not a finished data product. Values are sourced and dated; the group has not yet ratified the slate or the V1 proposal.

To be precise about the seam. V1, as the working documents define it, is data ingestion: raw and processed layers in native units, with source, vintage and licence stamped on every dataset, and no querying or scoring. The group has a V1 shipping proposal that awaits its ratification. This site is a separate thing beside that work. Its values are computed once, as a dated snapshot, from the named datasets over each region's recorded footprint; they are not the output of a live ingestion pipeline, there is no scheduled refresh, and there is no parcel-level resolution. The threshold sliders and the categorical filters demonstrate what filtering over sourced values looks like; they are not part of V1. V2, meaning automated querying, weights and red-line filtering, has not started, and any form of composite scoring is a deliberately deferred design question (see the tensions below). There are no user accounts and no collaboration features. Read as a demonstration, this page is honest; read as the finished tool, it would overclaim.

Three regional case studies

Three contrasting regions, read across the eight criteria.

Alentejo, Connemara, Transylvania. Hot-dry, mild-wet, continental-forested. Established regen networks, restrictive planning, post-communist commons. Each case study walks through what the place is, what the data says, and what a community arriving there would inherit. Numbers that come from the dashboard are the dashboard's own values. Land prices and legal rules date fast: every price and legal claim below carries its source inline or on the region's page, and anything older than a year is a starting point for your own check, not a quote. The case studies are Europe's; no North American region has one yet, and each region page carries its own Land standing and legal pathway.

Portugal · hot-dry Mediterranean

Alentejo

Cork-oak silvopasture on thin red ground, water-stressed to a 2050 ratio of 1.505, anchored by thirty years of Tamera.

By July the cork-oak shade in inland Alentejo stops being scenery and starts being infrastructure. Settlements here either build around what the trees already do, collecting deep shade in southwest corners, channelling air through stone, or design against the climate and lose ground every decade. The light at five in the afternoon is heavy and slow, the cicadas constant, the soil red where the stone breaks through. This is the dry southwest corner of the Iberian Peninsula: 41 people per square kilometre over the footprint (GHSL epoch 2020), a flat-roofed landscape of cork and holm oak, wheat stubble, and reservoirs that absorb rivers.

The current climate is hot-summer Mediterranean (Csa); by 2041–2060 the WorldClim CMIP6 v2.1 multi-model ensemble centres on 18.3°C mean annual temperature under SSP2-4.5, 2°C above 1970–2000. Annual rainfall already sits in a 500–650 mm envelope concentrated between October and April; high-resolution CMIP6 work for southern Portugal trims wet-day frequency another 10–15% by mid-century (Soares et al., Scientific Reports 2024). WRI Aqueduct 4.0's 2050 business-as-usual ratio over the footprint's sub-basins is 1.505, in WRI's class "extremely high" (above 0.8), and individual sub-basins run from 0.14 to 2.44. Soils are thin Cambisols and Luvisols on schist and granite; SoilGrids 2.0 (2020 release) reads 24.8 g/kg organic carbon over the 0–30 cm layer of the footprint and 46.7 g/kg in the 0–5 cm layer, which SoilGrids' own bands call moderate. The dominant living landscape is the montado: cork-oak and holm-oak silvopasture, listed as EU Habitats Directive habitat 6310, a working ecosystem with its own long grammar of shade, grazing and bark.

The Alentejo is thinly settled and its population is ageing (INE Censos 2021, Pordata 2023). Schools close, retirees arrive: French, German, Dutch, Belgian. Lisbon airport sits 1.5–2.5 hours from most of the region; Évora, Beja and Odemira anchor the regional hospital and school networks. On who may buy: the Civil Code gives foreigners the same civil rights as nationals unless a law says otherwise (art. 14, with a reciprocity clause), and the sources opened for the region's legal pathway found no nationality bar on owning rural land. Buying rural land does not carry a residence permit in those sources: Lei 56/2023, art. 42, stopped accepting new applications for the investment-based residence permits it names, the real-estate-linked routes, while permits already granted can be renewed (Lei 56/2023, Diário da República). No official regional price series was opened for the Alentejo, so no price band is printed here; the land-cost record on the region page carries the market-report range it rests on and says that it is not verified. The trap is assuming a purchase implies water rights or buildable area. In the Reserva Agrícola Nacional (RAN) new construction is prohibited except for listed uses, among them a permanent home for a farmer on the holding or for owners in proven economic need, and in the Reserva Ecológica Nacional (REN) building is prohibited unless compatible with the protection aims (DL 73/2009 arts. 21–22; DL 166/2008 on the REN). Both protections can sit on land that can still be bought freely, so establish classification and the applicable municipal plan before price.

Tamera, whose project began in 1978 and which chose its site in the Alentejo in 1995 (its own history page), describes itself as a community of around 160 and gives the region an internationally visible regen anchor; the EEA lists its water-retention landscape as a case study (Climate-ADAPT). Around it, Aldeia das Amoreiras, a centuries-old village of under 200 people in Odemira, was the subject of a village-wide permaculture design by the Centro de Convergência and GAIA (Permaculture Global); whether that work is active today could not be confirmed. The Living Atlas wave-1 count of regenerative projects within 100 km of the region's marker is 6 sites, a floor and not a census, among them Tamera, The Quinta Project and Traditional Dream Factory. National funding flows through PEPAC-Portugal (the CAP Strategic Plan 2023–2027), Fundo Ambiental ecological-restoration calls, and a small but real impact-VC layer (Maze Impact in Lisbon). The cork-oak agroforestry tradition (an EU Habitats Directive 6310 habitat) is the deeper regen lineage, older than the foreign newcomers, with its own grammar of bark cycles and grazing rhythms that any project putting down roots here ignores at its cost.

Temperature continues to climb. Plant-available water modelled out to 2050 contracts by 15–25% under SSP2-4.5 (Pereira et al., Journal of Water and Climate Change 2024). Heat-related mortality rises sharply on the present trajectory (Gomes et al., PMC11662523). The PV resource is generous: the footprint mean is 1,643 kWh/kWp/yr PVOUT (Global Solar Atlas, long-term average). That is modelled optimum-tilt potential, not realised yield. Portuguese self-consumption law (DL 162/2019 as amended 2022) makes shared-storage community-energy structures straightforward; energy abundance is not the problem. Water is. The post-Alqueva irrigation boom continues to draw down aquifers in sub-basins that Aqueduct already flags as stressed (EASAC 2020). UCDP records 0 conflict events within 200 km of the marker for 2019–2024, the nearest 749 km away. The trajectory is hotter, drier, quiet on the conflict record and increasingly attractive to mobile capital. The settlement question is whether the rainwater-catchment maths still closes by 2070.

Whose land it is: the region's Land standing entry reads it as a montado landscape of large herdade estates and sparse, ageing villages, and its entry route runs through the village and not around it. That entry, and the sources behind it, are on the region page.

What this region asks of you Alentejo asks for design from the rainwater equation backward. It asks for shade as infrastructure, for cisterns sized for six summer months on roof catchment, for dry toilets and greywater loops not as gestures but as load-bearing systems. It asks for patience with thirty-year-old neighbours in Tamera and three-hundred-year-old neighbours in the village. And it asks for honesty about what the next fifty Augusts will be, because the projects that mistake July 2025 for July 2075 will not survive the difference.

Ireland · mild-wet Atlantic

West Cork & Connemara

Blanket-peat carbon stores, a mild and wet climate trajectory, and a planning regime tighter than the open land suggests.

The Atlantic wind in Connemara doesn't move air so much as it moves time, pressure systems rolling east from the open ocean in eight- or twelve-hour cycles, the sky redoing itself before lunch. Light is silver and unstable. The Twelve Bens rise from a saturated peat plain that holds water like a sponge held under a tap, and the colour palette is mostly weather: dark mountain, paler bog, grey lake, gorse-yellow in spring. This is the west of Ireland: 33 people per square kilometre over the footprint (GHSL epoch 2020), Atlantic-maritime air, two-thousand-millimetre rainfall years, and a landscape so different from continental Europe that the criteria themselves shift weight.

The dashboard value for projected mean annual temperature in 2041–2060 is 11.1°C under SSP2-4.5, 1.3°C above 1970–2000, a smaller change than either of the other two case studies. The Irish national projections put the western counties at 11.0–11.8°C (EPA Research 471 / ICHEC TRANSLATE, 2023). Rainfall sits at 1,200 mm in the West Cork lowlands and rises past 2,400 mm into the Twelve Bens; two hundred or more rain-days a year are typical (Met Éireann 1991–2020 normals). WRI Aqueduct 4.0's 2050 business-as-usual ratio over the footprint is 0.011, in WRI's lowest class (below 0.1). The dominant soil is blanket peat: SoilGrids 2.0 reads 87.5 g/kg organic carbon over the 0–30 cm layer of the footprint and 124.3 g/kg in the 0–5 cm layer. Ireland's bogs hold roughly 53–62% of the national soil organic carbon on about a fifth of the land area, by EPA and Teagasc estimates with recent Sentinel-2 refinements (2024).

West Cork has received incomers continuously since the 1970s. Connemara's Gaeltacht core operates socially and educationally in Irish, and in the Galway Gaeltacht the county plan gives consideration to Irish speakers who prove competence and attaches a 15-year language enurement to schemes of two or more homes (Galway County Development Plan 2022–2028, chapter 4). On who may buy: the old statutory limit on acquiring land outside towns (Land Act 1965, section 45) was repealed by the Land Act 2005 according to the Irish Statute Book directory, and no replacement restriction on non-resident or foreign owners was found in the sources opened. Planning, not ownership, is the binding gate: both the Cork and Galway plans tie a one-off rural house to demonstrated rural links or need. Median agricultural land in 2024 was €10,914/acre (≈€26,970/ha) in the South-West (Cork and Kerry) region and €7,027/acre (≈€17,360/ha) in the West region (Galway, Mayo, Roscommon), CSO Ireland, Agricultural Land Prices 2024, released 31 October 2025.

Cloughjordan Ecovillage, a 67-acre site in Tipperary, describes itself as Ireland's first ecovillage; at about 91 km from the region's marker it is the one site in the Living Atlas wave-1 count within 100 km (1 site, a floor and not a census). Within West Cork itself, The Hollies Centre for Practical Sustainability at Enniskeane runs permaculture and ecobuild courses, and small-batch artisan dairying around Skibbereen and Castletownshend gives the food economy unusual depth. Connemara's regen presence is thinner but specific: Brigit's Garden at Roscahill, Hometree's native-woodland work in the west of Ireland, Atlantic Salmon Trust restoration on the Owenmore and Erriff catchments. The CAP Strategic Plan for Ireland (2023–2027), the ACRES agri-environment scheme, LEADER local-action groups and SEAI energy grants form the institutional stack.

Climate trajectory is mild. The risks are subtler than heat or drought: storm intensification reinforces the case for sheltered building placement and over-built roof structures, wetter winters favour fungal disease pressure on crops and structures, and the AMOC-shift tail risk is non-trivial on a multi-generational horizon, since IPCC AR6 keeps it low-probability but not negligible by 2100. Energy assumptions invert: PV alone reads 937 kWh/kWp/yr, about three-fifths of Alentejo's. Wind is not a criterion here, so the dashboard says nothing about it. The peat tension stays present: disturbing blanket bog releases century-deep carbon irreversibly on settlement timescales. UCDP records 0 conflict events within 200 km of the marker for 2019–2024, the nearest 261 km away. The trajectory is mild, wet and slowly drifting warmer, and the forest-cover reading is a loss, −3.2% per decade, not a recovery: Hansen's gain layer ends in 2012, its loss layer counts harvest as loss and regrowth after 2012 is not counted.

Whose land it is: the region's Land standing entry reads West Cork as rooted rural parishes and Connemara's Galway Gaeltacht as an Irish-speaking community, and says that lawful residence runs through county planning. That entry, and the sources behind it, are on the region page.

What this region asks of you Connemara asks for a different relationship with weight. Bog is not a foundation. Bog is a body of stored carbon that the wrong settlement design opens up like a wound. It asks for buildings that float rather than press, for drainage that respects watersheds rather than fights them, for a willingness to engage NPWS designations as live partners rather than paper obstructions. It asks newcomers to learn what the place was already doing, and in the Gaeltacht, to do that learning in Irish.

Romania · continental forested basin

Transylvania

Hay-meadow biodiversity that is exceptionally rich for the continent, large-carnivore Carpathians, and a peasant economy that the industrial wave largely missed.

A Saturday-morning hay meadow above Viscri in late June holds something between fifty and seventy plant species per square metre, a botanical density few places on the European continent match. The scythes still come out at dawn in the Saxon villages, the cattle still walk to common pasture behind a paid herdsman. Bears and wolves still live in the Făgăraș Mountains. This is the Transylvanian Plateau: a forested basin ringed by Carpathian crests at 300–900 m elevation, six languages within a day's drive, a still-living peasant economy that the EU's industrial-agriculture wave largely missed.

Current annual mean temperature sits at 8–10°C across the plateau, with continental winters bottoming at −10 to −15°C (ANM 1991–2020 normals). The mid-century projection under SSP2-4.5 is 9.3°C over the footprint, 3°C above 1970–2000, with summer warming concentrated at +2.5–3.0°C over the 1961–1990 baseline (Bojariu et al., Climate of the Romanian Carpathians, Springer 2014; CMIP6 via World Bank CCKP 2024). Rainfall runs 600–800 mm in lowlands and 1,000–1,400 mm in the surrounding ranges. WRI Aqueduct 4.0's 2050 business-as-usual ratio over the footprint is 0.415, in WRI's class "high" (0.4 to 0.8), and its sub-basins run from 0.02 to 0.68; local springs and hand-dug wells remain reliable. SoilGrids 2.0 reads 35.4 g/kg organic carbon over the 0–30 cm layer of the footprint, 63.4 g/kg in the 0–5 cm layer.

Population over the footprint reads 71 people per km² (GHSL epoch 2020), unevenly distributed: Cluj-Napoca has grown while many rural villages have lost population since 1990. The legal regime divides by land type. Law 17/2014 governs the sale of farmland outside built-up areas (extravilan) to Romanian, EU, EEA and Swiss citizens and to legal persons of those nationalities: a pre-emption queue in which relatives and co-owners, lessees, neighbours, young farmers and residents of the commune hold first claim during a 45-working-day public notice, then buyer tests (for a natural person who is not a pre-emption holder, five years' domicile or residence in Romania, farming activity and Romanian tax registration; for a company, a five-year seat, five years of farming and at least 75% of income from agriculture). Citizens of other states, and legal persons of a third state, may acquire such farmland only as international treaties provide, on a reciprocity basis (Law 17/2014 art. 2; Constitution art. 44(2)). Land inside built-up areas is outside Law 17/2014. Whether a Romanian company with non-EU shareholders counts as a Romanian legal person for Law 17/2014 was not found in an opened source, so no company route is described here. Arable land averages €8,656/ha nationally in 2024 (INS Romania 2024 release); that is a national figure and not a Transylvanian one.

Foundation Conservation Carpathia is restoring Carpathian forest as a private rewilding operation. The Mihai Eminescu Trust has long worked on Saxon-village heritage in Viscri, Mălâncrav and Floreni. ADEPT Foundation works the Hârtibaciu–Târnava Mare landscape on pasture restoration. Aurora Foundation at Stânceni in Mureș county anchors the intentional-community side; the Living Atlas wave-1 count within 100 km of the region's marker is 2 sites, a floor and not a census. Romania's CAP plan (PNS 2023–2027) carries dedicated high-nature-value grassland measures, and Rewilding Europe runs a Southern Carpathians programme. The deeper regen presence is the living one: most grassland is still mown by hand or with small machinery, and the practitioner skill that integrates here is closer to ecological stewardship than to reclamation: continue, don't restart.

Climate trajectory matters, but the starting point is cool enough that "hotter and drier" still leaves rain-fed agriculture, working forests and uncooled architecture viable, and growing seasons lengthen. AMOC-shift sensitivity is low: this is continental, not Atlantic. The under-weighted risk is forest-pathogen dynamics: bark beetle outbreaks in the spruce monocultures planted under Communist-era forestry are a present-tense issue, not a future one, and the forest-cover reading is a slight net loss, −1.5% per decade. Energy resource is moderate: PV reads 1,260 kWh/kWp/yr, small and micro-hydro suit the dense stream network, and firewood from sustainable forest yield remains the cultural and practical winter backbone. The political backdrop was tested in 2024 (the Constitutional Court's December 2024 ruling annulled the presidential election process), and UCDP records 0 conflict events within 200 km of the marker for 2019–2024, the nearest 281 km away.

Whose land it is: the region's Land standing entry reads the plateau as village commons, composesorat forest and pasture, kept by Romanian, Hungarian (Székely) and Roma communities and by the Saxon-heritage villages, and its entry route runs through the village hall's public pre-emption notice and through the commons. That entry, and the sources behind it, are on the region page.

What this region asks of you Transylvania asks for continuation rather than invention. The hay-meadow biodiversity holds because someone mows. The wood-pasture mosaic holds because someone grazes. The Saxon villages hold because someone keeps the roofs on. It asks the newcomer to learn an existing rhythm, the mowing calendar, the transhumance route, the composesorat membership and its closed doors, and to make a project that thickens that rhythm rather than replaces it. The project that thrives here is one the village can recognise as adjacent to its own.

Design tensions deferred to V2

Two questions the framework has chosen not to answer yet.

Both tensions below were debated at length inside the working group without resolution. The working documents are the group's own and are not published here; the tensions themselves are real intellectual content worth understanding before relying on the framework for any serious decision.

Composite scores.

The first tension is whether a regenerative-settlement framework should ever produce a single composite "livability" or "suitability" score across criteria.

One perspective

Never build a composite score. Any single number requires trade-off coefficients: how many millimetres of rainfall offset a thousand euros per hectare, how much cheap land offsets a thin civil-society stack. Once those coefficients live inside the framework, the framework is making the decision instead of the practitioner. A composite presents itself as objective when its inputs are arguments. Worse, it lets a region with one fatal flaw rank highly because its other scores compensate, which is precisely the failure mode for fifty-year decisions where no flaw is averageable.

The other perspective

The general principle is right but it overshoots. A domain-specific feasibility composite, for example a year-round household water budget that combines roof catchment, cistern volume, evapotranspiration and projected aquifer drawdown, is not the same as a livability composite. It collapses variables that are physically and dimensionally compatible and produces an answer to a single, well-defined question. A framework that refuses all composites loses the ability to answer those single questions clearly, and pushes the work onto each practitioner to reinvent the same calculation.

Weighting authority.

The second tension is about who decides how much a given criterion matters.

One perspective

Users should weight their own criteria. The framework provides values; the practitioner brings the value system. A family planning for children weights schools and healthcare differently than a hermit-settlement weights solitude. To embed weights inside the framework is to embed one project's values inside everyone else's decision. The framework's job is to lay out the data honestly, not to tell the viewer which dimensions to care about.

The other perspective

Some criteria are baseline non-negotiable, and the framework should resist drift toward "every preference is equally valid." Civil-society stability, for example, is not a preference: if a region's record on conflict or institutional stability fell below a threshold, no amount of personal weighting on cheap land should be allowed to override it. The framework loses its purpose if it lets users weight critical safeguards out of the picture entirely.

Both tensions are deliberately deferred to V2 design. This site, the demonstration and V1 alike, neither scores nor weights: it filters, and lets the viewer apply their own thresholds. The viewer who needs the framework to do more than that should treat it as a sketch and wait for the next layer, or read the sources listed below.

Ethics and values

The values this project is built on.

A framework about land carries questions a data set alone cannot answer. These are the values the project is built on — the commitments that shape what the tool is for, and what it refuses to be.

Localism. Decisions about land are made well primarily by people whose lives are continuous with the place. A tool that helps a stranger imagine a place cannot substitute for the slow work of becoming someone the place knows.

Bioregioning. Thinking in terms of watersheds, ecotones and cultural geographies rather than national borders and real-estate markets. A region can contain three watersheds and three different settlement traditions, and the bioregional scale is the scale that actually matters for land that is meant to last a century.

Reciprocity. Reciprocity is inseparable from bioregioning: you cannot live within the grain of a place while displacing the people already in it. Much of this land — Mi'kma'ki in Nova Scotia, the unceded Sinixt, Ktunaxa and Syilx territories of the Kootenays, the Zapotec, Mixtec and Chatino highlands of Oaxaca — is held in Indigenous or communal title, and arriving means entering obligation with the people, lifeways and nations already rooted there, not acquiring something empty. Where arriving in a place would harm the community already there, the honest answer is not to go. This tool exists to help people belong to and regenerate a place — never to land on a community already living there.

What that means in the tool. Every region page opens with whose land it is, in the words of the people where they have published them and in sourced facts where they have not. A region can carry a plain statement of the forms of arrival for which the answer is not there. First conversations lists public bodies to read, never people to contact. None of this is scored, ranked or used as a filter: a number would let a group buy down a people's standing against cheaper land.

Land standing is a qualitative record for each region: whose land it is, the tenure, how arriving in good faith works, and what that asks of an arrival, each with its sources (F-7, F-10, F-45). Assembled from public sources and checked on 5 October 2026. No nation or community named here has reviewed this entry. The words matter: an entry is checked against opened public sources, which is not review, consent or approval by anyone it names. The legal pathway block beside it lays out, in order, what lawful entry involves in the sources opened; it describes how the law and the process are laid out and is not legal advice, and where the law bars a structure it says so and offers no way round it (F-50, F-52).

"Not there" is a legitimate answer, never a gap to be worked around (F-42). In practice it is a short, sourced sentence on a region's page naming a form of arrival for which the answer is not there; nineteen of the thirty regions carry one. The demonstration also applies it before a region reaches the slate: regions where arriving would harm the community already there, and regions that are already amenity-migration hotspots, were left off, and none is named here. A region joins the slate only with complete sourced values, or an honest gap with its reason, and a Land standing entry checked against opened public sources (F-58). Where a region stays on the slate although one way of arriving there would harm, its page says so in that statement.

The bioregion layer is a descriptive reading and not a boundary. For each region it names the ecoregions (RESOLVE Ecoregions 2017) that overlap a disc of 100 km around the region's reference point, with shares rounded to five percent, and the river basin the water drains to. A disc is not the region's footprint, regions at an edge straddle ecoregions, and the polygons shown are simplified. Cultural bioregions, meaning the people, language and land tradition of a place, cannot be derived from a dataset: they are carried in prose, in Land standing and in what living there asks (F-54 to F-57).

The two continents are framed differently, on purpose (F-43). In North America an entry names the Indigenous nation or nations whose land it is, and what is known of its treaty and cession history, from the nations' own sources where they could be reached. In Europe it names the rooted community, the commons or the land tradition, a parish commons, a village assembly or a farm-family tenure, because that is usually the relationship in question, and it does not impose an Indigenous frame where the sources do not support one. The rule is to name who is there, in the terms they use, not to apply one frame everywhere.

The standing of those already rooted in a place is a gap in the group's four sovereignty axes, each of which describes the sovereignty of the people arriving. The group has been asked to consider a fifth consideration beside them, host-community standing, as a qualitative dimension and never a score. That is a proposal in the working documents: the group has not yet taken it up, and it changes no decision the group has committed to (F-40, F-44). The concern behind it is a mechanism, stated here as a judgement and not as a measured result: the conditions a settlement tool foregrounds, secure water, a mild climate and a lawful route, are the same conditions that draw buyers with outside income, so the tool's own targets may be one variable with the pressure that raises land prices against local incomes. No displacement figure is printed on this site.

How to arrive well, for people still weighing it, is at How to arrive. For people already in a place, For people already here says what this site says about your place and what it will not do.

Healthy integration. Whether a place welcomes new arrivals, and on what terms, is a matter of trust built across decades. It is not a checkbox a framework can verify. The work of integration sits with the arriving person, in relationship with the people already there.

Land stewardship. Stewardship as relationship over time, not transaction. Buying land is the easy part; becoming responsible to it over decades is the work the framework cannot represent in a data layer.

The framework is one input among many, most of which live not in datasets but in conversations with the people who already know a place. These values are how the project keeps that conversation honest: the tool exists to help a community arrive in reciprocity and give back to a place over generations — and where it cannot be used that way, it should not be used.

What's coming next

What the framework is working toward, and what it isn't promising.

The slate now covers thirty regions, fifteen in Europe and fifteen in North America. Alentejo, West Cork / Connemara and Transylvania (explored in depth above) anchored the first European cut, and North America extends the framework to a second continent, from the arid Southwest to the maritime Pacific Northwest, across a far wider catalogue of land-tenure regimes: US fee-simple with agricultural-use zoning, Canadian agricultural-land reserves, Québec civil law and CPTAQ farmland protection, and Mexican ejido communal title. Regions were added by a slate-admission rule: a region is admitted only with complete sourced values, or an honest gap with its reason, and a Land standing entry checked against opened public sources; a region that could not meet it was dropped, never shipped half-done. The slate itself remains a proposal pending the working group's sign-off, and the group has not yet reviewed how it grew. The current regions are listed on the home page, in no meaningful order.

One story about versions. V1, as the working documents define it, is data ingestion, and the group's V1 shipping proposal awaits ratification. This site is a designed demonstration beside it, not V1. V2, meaning automated querying against a live pipeline, practitioner-set thresholds against live data and any weighting, has not started. A serious V2 would ingest WorldClim CMIP6, WRI Aqueduct, SoilGrids and the regional cadastres on a scheduled cadence and surface per-cell source and vintage in the interface; that is months of work for a small team, not a single-person sprint.

The framework was originated by Askja and is developed by a small working group of practitioners and researchers. The demonstration site was built by one member of that group, who holds the practitioner seat. Members are named by first name and role only, as in the working documents; no surnames, handles or contact details appear anywhere on the site. There is no public contact route yet; the working group will set one. The group's invitation, a PDF that explains the working group, the roles and how its documents travel, is public; the working documents themselves are the group's own and are not published here. Open data sources; no commercial product.

Further regional case studies, a fourth and a fifth, will be drafted as time allows, and the page will grow with the work.
